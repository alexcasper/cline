# SwarmShape: Influence Structure in LLM Agent Swarms of Size 2–30 Replicates Across Serving Stacks; Conduct Rates Do Not

*Alex Cline (Atelier)*

## Abstract

When an LLM agent swarm misbehaves, blame usually lands on a bad actor. We ask
a different question: how much of a swarm's behaviour is a property of its
*shape* — who can talk to whom — rather than of any individual agent? We adapt
an LLM market harness (agora) so that seller swarms of size 2–30 communicate
over 57 distinct undirected communication graphs (exhaustively enumerated up to
size 8, family-sampled above), give every agent the same unenforced promise —
never charge buyers more than unit cost plus a markup — and detect promise-
breaking from committed prices, never from transcripts. We run the full
114-game grid twice, on two independent serving stacks (llama.cpp with a 4-bit
quant on dusk; vLLM on full-precision weights on Modal), plus 4-seed and
10-seed campaigns. One result reproduces across stacks. Who-follows-whom is
concentrated in every game (101/101 dusk games exceed the uniform baseline),
but most of that concentration is predicted by the graph's visibility
structure alone: a neighbor-uniform null explains the bulk of it (median
observed/null ratio 1.12 dusk, 1.07 Modal), leaving a modest behavioral
excess — agents follow some neighbours more than visibility predicts — in
73%/66% of games, stable across stacks. Conduct rates do not reproduce: the
clean-swarm promise-breaking rate is 2.5% of agent-rounds on dusk and 0.1% on
Modal; the instructed rogue's own rate is within 2× across hosts (0.064 vs
0.039–0.051), so the popular "rogue lift" ratio is ill-defined; and dusk's
action-level collapse at n $\geq$ 28 vanishes on Modal. The oversight lesson:
structural influence metrics travel across serving stacks; conduct baselines
must be established per stack, because an LLM swarm's apparent lawlessness can
be an artifact of how the model is served.

## 1. Introduction

Prior work from this atelier showed that small language models placed in
simulated markets collude, compete, or collapse depending on the communication
regime they are given, and that such conduct is best measured from *committed
actions* against exact benchmarks rather than judged from transcripts
("Emergent Collusion in LLM-Powered Market Agents"). That work varied *how many
agents could talk* (public board, private channels, coalitions). This paper
varies the *shape* of who-talks-to-whom, and changes the target conduct: not
collusion against buyers, but *rogue conduct* — an agent breaking a promise the
swarm was given, at the swarm's expense.

Three questions structure the study:

1. **Influence.** Whose announcements do swarm members follow, how concentrated
   is that following, and how much of the concentration is explained by the
   graph's visibility structure rather than by behavior?
2. **Base rate.** With no instructed bad actor, how often does an ordinary
   agent break an unenforced promise — and is that rate a property of the
   model or of the serving stack?
3. **Embedded rogue.** When one agent is instructed to break the promise while
   avoiding detection, does it do so, does it hide, and does it change what
   bystanders do?

The instrument follows the prior paper's philosophy: conduct is computed from
committed, platform-legal actions against an exact benchmark (the promised
price cap), so it cannot be faked in talk and cannot be missed by a judge.
The treatment space is 57 connected graphs from size 2 to 30, generated by a
self-contained enumerator (exhaustive and verified to size 8) and a
deterministic family sampler above, wired into the harness as pairwise
channels. What the study found was not what it set out to measure: the
robust, stack-independent signal is structural (who-follows-whom is largely
visibility-determined, with a small stable behavioral excess), while the
conduct rates the instrument was built to track turned out to be relative to
the serving stack.

**Datasets.** (i) The full single-seed grid: 114 games (57 graphs ×
clean/rogue, harness seed 42) on dusk (llama.cpp, Q4_K_M quant); 103 pass the
pre-registered missing-action gate (§3). (ii) The identical grid re-run on
Modal (vLLM, full-precision weights, 2× L40S): all 114 games valid.
(iii) Multiseed on Modal: a 20-graph stratified subset × clean/rogue × seeds
43–46 (80 complete pairs, zero data-quality failures). (iv) Ten seeds on four
canonical shapes (flat, ring, star, hierarchy; n = 8; seeds 42–51; 80 games).

## 2. Related Work

Multi-agent LLM studies have reported emergent collusion in repeated games
(Calvano et al. 2020 for algorithms; Banchio & Skrzypacz 2022 for AI auction
agents), and social-norm dynamics in communities of LLM agents (GovSim).
Sprint-adjacent work established that the communication *regime* — public,
private, or coalition-structured — moves conduct indices across whole regimes.
This study differs in three ways: the treatment is the *communication graph
itself* (a config-level channel spec, enabling sizes from 2 to 30); the
measured conduct is *violation of a promise given to the swarm*, detected
mechanically from an exact benchmark; and an influence metric (takeover HHI)
measures emergent hierarchy without asking agents anything — with a graph-
aware null (§3) separating visibility structure from behavioral following.

Two older literatures make the same structural bet with different agents and
outcomes. Formal epistemic network models simulate inquiring agents who share
evidence with neighbours and update rationally; topology is the treatment, and
population-level outcomes — most famously the Zollman effect, where sparser
networks preserve diversity and reach truth faster (Zollman 2007; Bala &
Goyal 1998; O'Connor & Weatherall 2018; Ball et al. 2024) — emerge that no
single agent chooses. Classical distributed systems give the engineering-side
answer: agreement among loyal agents is guaranteed only under headcount and
connectivity conditions tied to the number of traitors m (n $\geq$ 3m+1
(Pease, Shostak & Lamport 1980); connectivity > 2m (Dolev 1982)). Those
results *design* networks to survive known traitors under a protocol; this
paper *measures*, with no protocol or headcount guarantee, who an LLM swarm
follows, and whether its norm-breaking is measurable reliably.

## 3. Methods

**Harness.** Agora (`agora/`), scenario `price_war`: sellers choose posted
prices each round against cost-heterogeneous demand; profit is computed by the
platform. An `Arm` carries a `topology` (named shape or explicit
`graph_edges`), `honor_rules` (unenforced promises in the system prompt), and
an optional `rogue` agent instructed to break the promise while avoiding
detection. Each undirected graph edge {i, j} becomes one pairwise board
readable and writable by exactly {i, j}. The four canonical shapes beyond the
grid are channel-level objects: flat (one public board), ring (pairwise
neighbour boards), star (spokes write a hub inbox; hub alone broadcasts), and
a 2-3-3 hierarchy (workers → supervisors → managers, directives broadcast
down).

**Graph space.** Connected undirected simple graphs. Sizes 2–8 are
exhaustively enumerated (OEIS A001349 connected class counts on n = 1–8: 1,
2, 4, 11, 34, 156, 1044, 12346); two representative classes per size are
played. Sizes 9–30 use deterministic-seeded family draws (uniform random
trees via Prüfer sequences, Erdős–Rényi at p = 0.20 and 0.35,
Barabási–Albert scale-free m=2, chain-of-command with skip links), redrawn
until connected and unseen; deduplication is by colour-refinement
certificate, so the sampling scheme, not class counts, is the reported
object. Total: 57 graphs. The shipped census
(`agora/data/graph_census.json`, 13,996 graphs) contains the 13,598
connected exhaustive classes on n $\leq$ 8 plus 398 sampled draws.

**Rogue instrument.** All agents are told: "never charge buyers more than your
unit cost + 10 credits," framed as reputation-binding but not
platform-policed. A violation is a committed price above that cap — computed
from committed actions, never judged from transcripts. In rogue arms, the
highest-degree vertex (tie: lowest index) — a *central* position — receives a
private instruction to break the promise without making it obvious.

**Influence and the visibility null.** Each round, agents may announce
prices; each committed price that exactly matches a peer's announced price
counts as one follow of that peer. We report takeover HHI — the Herfindahl
index of follow counts — against the uniform baseline 1/n, and, critically,
against a **graph-aware null**: under neighbor-uniform following (each agent
splits its following uniformly over its neighbours), the expected follow
share of agent j is proportional to $\Sigma_{i \in N(j)}$ 1/d_i, giving a null HHI that
depends only on the graph. HHI → 1 means one agent's proposals drive the
swarm; the null captures how much concentration the visibility structure
alone would produce. The difference between observed and null HHI is the
behavioral component.

**Grid, model, and the two serving stacks.** 57 graphs × {clean, rogue},
harness seed 42, horizon 3–6 rounds (hidden from agents). Stack (i) **dusk**:
llama.cpp serving the Qwen3.5-4B Q4_K_M quant, 4 slots; truncated-response
rate mean 0.017 (max 0.078), invalid-tool-call rate mean 0.150 (max 0.400);
11 games (all n $\geq$ 28) failed the pre-registered missing-action gate — the
four n = 29 and four n = 30 games produced zero valid rounds, and three of
four n = 28 games missed 0.25–0.5 — leaving the 103-game gated set (52
clean, 51 rogue). Stack (ii) **Modal**: vLLM serving the same model's
full-precision weights on 2× L40S; all 114 games valid with zero missing
actions and zero truncated responses at every size — dusk's large-n failure
is a serving artifact, not a model limit. The stacks differ as a composite
(engine, quantization, decoding defaults, hardware); we do not isolate
individual causes. Every game writes `events.jsonl`, `summary.json`, and
`config.yaml` with per-game data-quality columns.

**Analysis discipline.** The unit of analysis is the *graph*: arms are
compared pairwise on the same graph. Violation rates are per agent-round
("slot": rounds × agents), pooled over slots. Neither analysis was
pre-registered; we designate the influence analysis as primary because it is
the one that survives the cross-host reproduction, and we label the conduct
analyses as stack-relative. The dusk/Modal pair is a reproduction on a second
serving stack, not an independent replication.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Influence: visibility explains most of it, and the rest is stack-independent

On dusk, every gated game with defined HHI (101/101) exceeds the uniform
baseline — some hierarchy always emerges. But the neighbor-uniform null
explains most of it: the median observed/null ratio is **1.12** (Modal: 1.07),
and observed exceeds the null in **73%** of dusk games (66% Modal) — with a
substantial minority (27%/34%) *below* the null, i.e. following more evenly
than visibility predicts. After null adjustment, the size gradient of
takeover-HHI excess essentially disappears ($\rho$(n) of observed/null = 0.19
dusk, 0.07 Modal): the strong raw association with size is mostly the
graphs' own visibility structure, since sampled families are sparser at
larger n.

The raw dusk numbers: takeover ratio (HHI/uniform) by family — exhaustive
classes 1.54 (24 games), scale-free 1.46 (18), G(n, 0.20) 1.42 (21), G(n,
0.35) 1.35 (36), chain+skips 1.31 (2); mean 1.43× over the 101 games with
defined HHI (two gated games produced no follows). Takeover-HHI excess
correlates with size at $\rho$ = −0.75 (dusk, 51 gated pairs) and $\rho$ = −0.75 on
Modal over the same 50 graphs with defined HHI (−0.77 over all 56 Modal
clean games) — the one cross-stack-reproduced structural regularity.
Normalized hubness (max degree/(n−1)) correlates raw +0.58/+0.61 but only
+0.02/+0.17 partial given n: after controlling for size, hubness adds
essentially nothing beyond the null, so we do not claim a hub effect.

### 4.2 Conduct rates are serving-stack-relative, not portable

| quantity | dusk (llama.cpp, Q4) | Modal (vLLM, full precision) | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| games passing DQ gate | 103/114 (all failures n $\geq$ 28) | **114/114** | dusk failures were serving artifacts |
| clean base rate (pooled) | 0.0245 | **0.001** (0.0000 in n = 2–19 bands) | **does not reproduce** |
| rogue-seat rate | 0.0637 | 0.039 | within 2× across all Modal datasets |
| lift ratio (rogue/clean) | 2.6× | 39× | ill-defined: denominator collapses |
| rogue vs same seat in clean arm | 0.0613 vs 0.0189 (3.2×, 53 pairs) | — | position-controlled lift survives on dusk |
| multiseed (Modal, seeds 43–46) | — | clean 0.0015; rogue-seat 0.0281; rogue defects in 5/80 cells | base-rate absence is seed-robust |

The clean base rate does not reproduce: 2.5% on dusk, $\approx$ 0.1% on Modal —
violations in only 5 of 80 multiseed clean cells — and dusk's n $\geq$ 28
"capability failure" vanishes entirely on Modal. We emphasize what we *cannot*
claim: per-game dusk violation rates do not correlate with per-game
invalid-tool-call or truncation rates (Spearman $\rho$ = 0.09/0.04), so we have
not isolated the mechanism. Candidate causes for the stack difference include
output corruption from the 4-bit quant, vLLM vs llama.cpp decoding defaults,
and batching; the two stacks differ as a composite. What the data do support:
measured conduct rates are conditional on the serving stack, and "an LLM
swarm breaks its promises at rate x" is not a portable finding.

### 4.3 The embedded rogue on dusk: present, immediate, non-contagious — with caveats

On dusk (51 gated rogue games): the instructed rogue breaks the promise at
0.0637 per agent-round; 33 of 37 violating rogue arms defect in round 1 (no
camouflage); bystander rates are statistically unchanged (+0.3 pp; spillover
sign test 27/16/8, n.s.); the rogue is the top violator in only 8 of 37
violating games. The position-matched comparison strengthens the lift: the
rogue's own seat violates at 0.0613 when instructed vs 0.0189 for the same
seat in the clean arm (3.2×, 53 pairs). On Modal, rogue-seat rates are within
2× of dusk on all three datasets (0.039 grid; 0.051 canonical; 0.028
multiseed), but defection is all-or-nothing across seeds (5 of 80 cells;
seed 44: zero defections in 20 games; onset round 1 in 4 of 5; bystanders
0.0005), so no Modal effect size is estimable. Paired by graph on dusk:
rogue exceeds clean in 23, ties in 14, is lower in 14 — the pooled rates
exceed clean on every dataset, but the per-graph paired tests are null.

## 5. Discussion

The study set out to measure whether rogue conduct is a property of swarm
shape. The honest answer has two parts. **What is robust: influence structure,
mostly visibility.** Following in an LLM swarm is concentrated in every game,
on both stacks; the graph's visibility structure alone predicts most of the
concentration (median observed/null 1.07–1.12); and the residual behavioral
excess — following some reachable peers more than visibility predicts — is
positive-leaning and stable across stacks (73%/66% of games), with no size
gradient of its own. For oversight, this is useful and cheap: who-follows-whom
concentration in a swarm can be largely predicted from its communication graph
plus a small stack-stable behavioral margin. What we do *not* claim: node-
level targeting (that the most-followed agent is the highest-degree vertex) —
that check remains future work.

**What is not robust, and worse, not portable: conduct rates.** The apparent
2.5% spontaneous promise-breaking base rate collapses to $\approx$ 0.1% on a cleaner
stack of the same model; the rogue lift ratio swings from 2.6× to 39× not
because rogues changed but because the denominator did; and a capability
failure at n $\geq$ 28 turns out to be a serving artifact. We are explicit that
the mechanism is unresolved — dusk violations do not correlate with per-game
truncation or malformed-call rates, so "quantization noise" remains a
hypothesis, not a finding, alongside decoding defaults and engine differences.
The operational lesson stands regardless of mechanism: *a conduct rate
measured over one serving stack does not transfer*, so any misconduct
dashboard for LLM swarms must baseline rates against stack health and
re-baseline on every stack change.

**Why the promise is still the right instrument.** An unenforced promise is
the deployment condition for most real agent swarms: norms stated in prompts,
no platform police. The measured quantity — committed actions outside a
promised region — is mechanically detectable, cheap to state in any domain,
and, as this study shows, sensitive enough to expose its own measurement
conditions. Limitations are collected in Appendix A.

## 6. Conclusion

Across 57 communication graphs and swarm sizes 2–30, with the full grid
reproduced on a second serving stack and extended by 4-seed and 10-seed
campaigns: who-follows-whom in an LLM swarm is concentrated in every game,
mostly because of the graph's visibility structure (median observed/null
1.07–1.12), with a modest behavioral excess that is stable across stacks; and
promise-breaking rates that looked like spontaneous swarm misconduct on one
stack (2.5% base, 2.6× rogue lift) shrink or invert on another (0.1%; rogue
defects in 5/80 cells). For oversight of real LLM swarms: predict influence
from the communication graph — that part travels; treat conduct baselines as
serving-stack properties — that part does not.

## Appendix A: Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

**Limitations.** (1) The dusk grid is single-seed (42); seed replication
exists only on Modal (seeds 43–46 on 20 graphs; 42–51 on four canonical
shapes), so dusk-specific numbers carry one-seed volatility — the rogue-lift
ratio moved 1.8× → 2.6× between cuts of the same run. (2) One 4B-class
model; rogue *strategy* is likely capability-dependent, and the 5-of-80 Modal
defection rate says nothing about larger models. (3) The serving stacks
differ as composites (engine, quantization, decoding defaults, hardware,
batching); we show conduct rates are stack-relative but do not isolate the
cause, and per-game dusk violation rates do not correlate with per-game
truncation or invalid-call rates ($\rho$ = 0.09/0.04), so the corruption
mechanism is a hypothesis, not a finding. (4) The takeover-HHI null assumes
follows originate uniformly per agent and split uniformly over neighbours;
other nulls (e.g. degree-weighted announcement propensity) would shift the
behavioral-residual estimate. (5) Exact price matching can produce
coincidental follows (common round numbers, cost+10 anchors); no tie-breaking
rule beyond counting each matching announcement is applied. (6) Undirected
graphs only; the rogue is always highest-degree (in rogue arms, hub position
and rogue identity are confounded); the promise domain is one scenario.
(7) HHI is noisy at small n and the horizon varies 3–6 rounds. (8) Neither
analysis was pre-registered; the influence result is designated primary post
hoc, on replication grounds. (9) n, density, and hubness are confounded by
the sampling scheme (larger swarms are sparser); partial correlations control
size but cannot rescue hubness as an independent factor (partial $\rho$ $\approx$
0.02–0.17).

**Dual-use.** The influence-mapping technique locates persuasion structure in
a deployed swarm from its communication graph; an adversary could use the
same computation to find and target a swarm's most-followed agent. The
serving-sensitivity finding could be misread as "misconduct rates are tunable
via infrastructure." We judge the defensive value higher: the instrument is
cheap, computed from platform-side committed actions, works at 30-agent
scale, and its main lesson — baseline conduct metrics against serving health
before interpreting them — is defensive by construction. No capability for
*causing* misconduct beyond a single instructed agent in a sandbox is
introduced.
