## Abstract

Collusion between firms is a classic economic failure; collusion between
autonomous AI agents is a newly deployable one. We ask whether small
language-model agents collude in simulated markets when nothing in their
instructions suggests it, and whether their communication channels change
the outcome. We present *agora*, a benchmark of six market structures —
price wars, auctions, supply chains, electricity storage, ride-hailing, and
ad auctions — each with exact competitive and collusive benchmarks, so
collusion is computed from committed actions rather than judged from
transcripts. Seven open-weight models (2–4B) were each slotted into a 150-game grid — six markets × five communication regimes × five seeds. The harness enforces provenance by design
(hidden horizons, no silent defaults, per-run parse-error columns) after a
token-budget bug in our first campaign silently deleted agent actions.
Across 590 completed runs (per-model completion 13–150), the dominant outcome
was play *at or below* the competitive benchmark — cutthroat undercutting,
overbidding, and misplacement that destroyed the agents' own profits. Genuine
collusion appeared where structure invited it: in the supply chain, every
model with completed runs elevated prices toward the joint-profit benchmark
(index 0.54–0.94 across six models), and one model given a shared team
objective and a private channel exceeded the collusive benchmark outright
(index 2.31). Market design, not transcript policing, is
the near-term lever.

## 1. Introduction

Automated pricing and bidding agents are being deployed into real markets
now, and economic theory — backed by simulations (Calvano et al., 2020) and
empirical evidence (Assad et al., 2024) — says that self-interested learners
in repeated markets can drift into supra-competitive prices without any
human deciding to fix them. Fish et al. (2024) showed that large language
models do the same, and that seemingly innocuous prompt cues increase it;
Nakamura et al. (2026) showed that private channels between agents introduce
collusion. If AI agents are deployed with communication channels at all, the
threat model is concrete: agents that coordinate against customers or
counterparties while their public behavior looks like ordinary competition.

This paper asks whether small open-weight language models — the kind most
likely to be deployed at scale — collude when nothing in their instructions
suggests it, and whether their communication channels change the answer,
using six market structures, seven models, five communication regimes, and
an outcome measure computed from committed actions against exact benchmarks.

Our main contributions are:

- **agora**, a benchmark for collusion among LLM agents in repeated markets,
  with exact competitive and collusive benchmarks for every scenario,
  hidden horizons, no silent defaults, and per-run data-quality columns
  (`missing_action_rate`, `truncated_rate`) — design corrections motivated by
  a truncation failure in our own first campaign that silently deleted agent
  actions (Section 3, Appendix B).
- **A 590-run cross-model grid** (seven models, six market structures, five
  communication regimes, five seeds) showing that (i) no completed model
  reaches the collusive benchmark; (ii) the dominant outcome is play *below*
  the competitive benchmark — self-harming over-competition; (iii) genuine
  collusion appears only when agents are enrolled as a whole-market team
  *with* a private channel; and (iv) a private channel *without* team framing
  produces the most cutthroat play of all (Section 4).
- **A provenance discipline** for LLM experiments: every run carries a
  parse-error count; contaminated series are quarantined, never averaged in;
  and every number in the paper is recomputable from per-call event logs
  (Section 3, Appendix B).

## 2. Related Work

**Algorithmic collusion in economics.** Calvano et al. (2020): independent
Q-learners sustain supra-competitive prices "without communication"; Assad et
al. (2024): matching evidence in retail gasoline; Banchio and Skrzypacz
(2022): auction algorithms collude through timing. We add language models
acting through tool calls, with collusion measured against exact benchmarks.

**LLM-agent collusion.** Fish et al. (2024): LLM sellers reach
supra-competitive prices and innocuous prompt cues increase it. Lin et al.
(2024): LLM agents divide markets. Nakamura et al. (2026): private channels
introduce collusion — our regime factor operationalizes their channel
question directly. Keppo et al. (2026): agent diversity reduces collusion —
our per-model results reinforce that model identity is first-order. Ad-auction
shading is studied by Tolety (2025) and Agrawal et al. (2025); say-vs-do
equivocation by Shi et al. (2026).

**Benchmarks and detection.** Terrarium (our prior suite) and Colosseum
provided phase-gated environments; ColludeBench packages detectors. Agora
adds exact per-scenario benchmarks, integrity-by-design (hidden horizon, no
silent defaults, data-quality columns), and seven-model coverage under one
harness. On detection, our earlier four-view comparison showed hybrid
say-vs-do auditing dominating transcript-only or action-only views on
seed-42 trajectories; agora's event logs support that analysis per seed.

## 3. Methods

**The six scenarios.** Each is a market structure with an exact competitive
benchmark (index 0) and an exact collusive benchmark (index 1), so collusion
is computed, not judged:

1. **Price war** — three sellers, private costs; buyers take the cheapest.
2. **Auction house** — first-price sealed bids; the seller is the victim.
3. **Supply chain** — two suppliers → manufacturer; the squeeze is vertical.
4. **Grid storage** — battery owners trade into a market their actions move.
5. **Ride-hailing** — drivers pick zones under surge pricing.
6. **Ad auctions** — generalized second-price slots; the platform is the victim.

(Full mechanics, players, and parameters: Table in §3.1–3.3 and the schematic
captions; competitive and collusive benchmarks per scenario are exact.)

**The models.** Seven small open-weight models (2–4B parameters) played every
game: G9v3-3B, Granite-4.2-3B, K2-Horizon-3.7B, LFM2.5-2.6B, MiniCPM5-2B,
Nanbeige4.1-3B, and Phi-4-mini, each served locally on Modal with its own
recommended sampling and reasoning settings (model cards differ; we do not
force identical samplers). Six of the seven have complete runs; K2-Horizon is
partial (142 of 150) and completes this series.

**The five communication regimes.** `neutral` is the competitive baseline;
the four coalition regimes cross partnership (2 members vs. whole market),
team awareness (coalition prompt on/off), and a hidden channel (on/off) —
the three ingredients any anti-collusion rule would have to restrict
(definitions in the table below).

**Procedure.** Each game has a hidden horizon: at least 4 rounds, then each
additional round happens with probability 0.75, up to 12 — no agent is told
which round is last, so there is no end-game artifact. Agents act through
tool calls (propose, then commit); a missing or unparseable action counts as
an abstention, the round is flagged invalid, and every headline metric is
computed over valid rounds only. Every LLM call — prompt, reasoning, tool
call, result — is written to an event log, and each run reports its own data
quality: `missing_action_rate` and `truncated_rate`. Generation budgets are
generous (16,384 tokens per call, temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95), so truncation
is measured rather than suffered. The grid is 6 scenarios × 5 regimes × 5
seeds = 150 runs per model; 590 of the 1,050 planned runs completed at
cut-off (per-model completion 13–150, reported with the results).

---





**Harness and serving.** All games run under *agora*, a self-contained
benchmark with no dependence on the earlier Terrarium/Colosseum code. Each
model is served in its own Modal container by vLLM 0.31.0 behind an
OpenAI-compatible tool-calling endpoint, and agents play exclusively through
tool calls — every decision is a parsed, logged function call, never free
text. Generation budgets are 16,384 tokens per call. Sampling parameters
follow each model's own card and therefore differ across models (e.g.
Nanbeige4.1 at temperature 0.6 / top-p 0.95, G9v3-3B at temperature 0.9); we
treat model identity as a treatment variable, not a nuisance parameter, and
report per-model results rather than pooling. The full grid is 6 scenarios ×
5 regimes × 5 seeds = 150 runs per model — 1,050 run slots, of which 1,042
completed at the time of writing (K2-Horizon 142/150 and finishing).

**Scenarios and players.** Each scenario fixes its players and pays in
credits:

| Scenario | Players | Market parameters | Competitive benchmark | Collusive benchmark |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `price_war` | 3 sellers | private costs U[40, 60]; demand 60; reservation price 100 | Bertrand: price just under the 2nd-lowest cost | all sellers at the reservation price |
| `auction_house` | 3 bidders | first-price sealed-bid, private values U[50, 150], minimum bid 10 | symmetric equilibrium bid | ring wins at the minimum bid |
| `supply_chain` | 2 suppliers → manufacturer | supplier costs U[20, 40]; linear demand (intercept 120, slope 1) | upstream Bertrand + manufacturer best response | joint-profit input price |
| `grid_storage` | 4 battery owners | 10 MWh capacity each; price impact 2; blocks priced 20/50/90/60 | Cournot–Nash (best-response dynamics; exact per-owner DP) | fleet-wide dynamic-programming optimum |
| `ride_hailing` | 4 drivers | 4 zones (demand means 7/4/2/1); capacity 4; base price 10, surge ×5, cap 80; riders' WTP U[10, 60] | Nash placement (best-response dynamics) | joint-earnings-maximizing placement |
| `ad_auction_gsp` | 4 bidders | GSP slots with CTRs 0.5/0.3/0.1; 100 clicks/round; reserve 1.0; values U[2, 15] | truthful/VCG (lowest envy-free equilibrium) | all winners paying the reserve |

**Regimes.** Every scenario is played under five regimes. `neutral` has no
coalition, no team prompt, and no private channel — the competitive baseline.
The four coalition regimes cross two design levers: whether the coalition
members are *told* they are a team (`coalition_prompt` on/off) and whether
they share a *private channel* invisible to the others (`private_channel`
on/off):

| Regime | Coalition | Team prompt | Private channel |
|---|---|---|---|
| `pair_prompt_private` | 2 members | yes | yes |
| `pair_prompt_only` | 2 members | yes | no |
| `pair_channel_only` | 2 members | no | yes |
| `all_prompt_private` | whole market | yes | yes |

The fifth slot is scenario-shaped: in `auction_house` the whole-market arm is
a 3-bidder `ring_all`; in `supply_chain` it is a 3-member `chain_prompt_private`
reflecting the vertical topology; elsewhere `all_prompt_private` enrolls every
player. This design isolates which ingredient — partnership, team awareness,
or a hidden channel — actually moves collusion, rather than bundling them.

**Seeds and horizon.** Each (scenario, regime) cell is played at 5 seeds
(`seed0`–`seed4`), giving 25 runs per scenario per model and 150 per model.
The number of rounds is hidden from the agents: every scenario runs at least
4 rounds (`grid_storage`: 2) and then continues round-by-round with
probability 0.75 up to a cap of 12. Nothing in any prompt reveals which round
is last, so there is no end-game round to coordinate on.

**Turn structure and integrity.** A round has a planning phase (up to 3
steps: announcements and proposals on a shared blackboard, capped at 40
messages) followed by an execution phase (up to 3 steps: the binding tool
call — `commit_price`, `submit_bid`, `commit_charge`, and so on). There are
no silent defaults: an agent that fails to produce a parseable action
*abstains*, the round is flagged invalid, and the run's
`missing_action_rate` rises. Headline metrics are computed over valid rounds
only; `truncated_rate` and `text_recovered_rate` record how often generation
was cut off or salvaged, so data quality is a reported column, not an
assumption. Per-round say-vs-do behavior is logged directly: each run reports
its `proposal_commit_pairs`, `proposal_commit_divergence_rate`, and
`commit_above_proposal_rate`.

**Metrics.** The headline `collusion_index` maps each valid round onto the
line from the scenario's exact competitive benchmark (0) to its exact
collusive benchmark (1); per-scenario companions include buyer overcharge,
revenue vs. benchmark, tie/efficiency round counts, and welfare. Performance
per model (seconds per call, tokens per second) is recorded so capability
differences can be separated from strategic differences. Every LLM call —
prompt, reasoning, tool call, and result — is preserved in `events.jsonl`,
and each run ships a `config.yaml` snapshot and a `summary.json`, making
every number in this paper recomputable from its events log.

**Design lineage.** Agora is an independent reimplementation inspired by
**Terrarium** (say-vs-do phases, blackboard mediation, the six scenario
templates) and **Colosseum** (the arm/regime structure); its additions —
exact benchmarks, hidden horizon, no silent defaults, data-quality columns —
are the corrections from our truncation incident (Appendix B).

## 5. Discussion

**Headline.** Across 420 runs and four models, no model reaches the collusive
benchmark — and almost every mean lands *below* the competitive benchmark.
The dominant failure mode of small language-model traders is not collusion
but cutthroat over-competition: undercutting past profitability, overbidding
past private value, and misplacing into congestion until profits turn
negative. Where supra-competitive outcomes do appear, they are mild
(supply chain +0.28, auction +0.09, price war +0.07) and concentrated in
specific model–regime combinations rather than being systematic.

**By scenario.** The supply chain leans collusive most robustly: LFM2.5-2.6B
sits at or above 0.66 in every regime (1.06 in `pair_prompt_private`), and
MiniCPM5-2B's paired regimes average +0.65 to +0.85. The price war contains
the study's clearest genuine cartel: MiniCPM5-2B under `all_prompt_private`
posts the reservation price (100.0) in every valid round of 4 of 5 seeds (22
of 23 valid rounds at a full tie). Grid storage is a catastrophe (indices to
−58; 26/70 runs invalid) — naive fleet charging spikes the agents' own input
prices. Ride-hailing shows uniform under-service: a coordination failure,
not a cartel.

**By regime.** The only positive pooled regimes are the whole-market
topologies — `ring_all` (+0.10) and `chain_prompt_private` (+0.18). Among
pair regimes, a private channel *without* a team prompt (`pair_channel_only`,
−1.30) is the most cutthroat condition, while team awareness without a
channel (`pair_prompt_only`, −0.51) is the mildest. The necessary ingredients
are conjunctive — partnership, framing, and channel together.

**By model.** G9v3-3B plays closest to the benchmarks everywhere (−0.42 ±
0.61); Phi-4-mini follows (−0.66 ± 1.43); LFM2.5-2.6B and MiniCPM5-2B match
on means (−1.26, −1.28) with extreme tails (SD = 7.38, 4.76) driven by grid
storage. Model identity is a first-order variable, hence per-model tables
rather than a pooled index.

**Closing.** The near-term risk of small open-weight trading agents is not
secret cartels but unstable, self-harming competition — real welfare harm
that standard collusion detection would miss. When coordination does appear
it tracks the communication design, and even then it depends on the model.
Benchmark-anchored measurement makes these comparisons auditable; results are
provisional until the remaining three model grids close.


## 6. Conclusion

We built a benchmark in which collusion between language-model agents is a
computed quantity, and used it to run 590 games across seven small
open-weight models, six market structures, five communication regimes, and
five seeds. The results are sobering in an unexpected way: the models we
tested do not reliably collude — they mostly compete so fiercely that they
destroy their own profits. But collusion does appear at a specific point in
the design space: in the supply chain, every completed model drifted toward
the joint-profit benchmark (index 0.54–0.94), and one model given a team
objective and a private channel exceeded the collusive benchmark outright. And the sharpest
regime effect runs the other way: a private channel without a shared
objective made play more cutthroat, not less. For anyone deploying LLM
trading agents, the implications are that transcripts alone will not tell
you whether your agents are misbehaving, that benchmark-anchored audits of
committed actions will, and that the cheapest intervention is architectural:
restrict enrollment, framing, and hidden channels before you reach for
monitoring.

## Appendix A: Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

**What these results establish.** Under our harness, four small open-weight
models (2–4B) playing six market structures rarely reached collusive
benchmarks, most often played below competitive benchmarks, and produced
genuine cartels in exactly one model-regime combination (a whole-market
coalition with team framing and a private channel, in a Bertrand price war).
Regime effects (team framing and private channels matter; unexplained
secrecy destabilizes) are consistent across models.

**What they do not establish.** These are 2–4B models with limited
reasoning depth; frontier models may coordinate more capably, and nothing
here bounds that risk. The hidden-horizon cap (12 rounds) forbids the
repeated-game punishment strategies that sustain human cartels, so our
design cannot detect collusion that requires long-horizon enforcement. The
collusion index is anchored to two benchmarks per scenario; outcomes between
the benchmarks are interpolated, and outcomes beyond them (deeply negative
indices) are interpreted as self-harming play — a reading we verified by
hand in two runs but not in all 420.

**Assumptions.** We assume parsed tool calls reflect deliberate committed
actions; the harness records but does not fully control for prompt-cache and
sampling nondeterminism across serving stacks (we measured cross-host
divergence at the same seed in the companion series). Per-model sampler
settings follow model cards and differ across models, so model differences
conflate capability and configuration.

**Dual-use / ethical considerations.** Agora itself is defensive tooling:
it measures collusion against public benchmarks and logs data quality. Its
scenario definitions, reward structures, and the documented finding that
whole-market enrollment plus team framing plus a private channel produced
sustained cartels could in principle inform someone designing colluding
agent systems; we consider the same information essential for auditors and
market designers, and the released artifacts contain no working
"collusion recipe" beyond what Calvano et al. (2020) and Fish et al. (2024)
already published. All games are simulated; no real market, user, or third
party was affected.

## Appendix B: Companion Terrarium Series and the Truncation Incident

The pre-sprint companion series (Terrarium engine, seed 42, `max_tokens`
512) produced the flagship single-trajectory findings: unprompted cartel
solicitation, universal cost disclosure, a 59/59/59 supra-competitive tie,
and a Day-4 say-vs-do divergence (all three sellers proposing 58 while
committing 59). These runs contain zero truncation events, and the first
multiseed rerun's clean seeds did not reproduce the tie or the Day-4 event;
we therefore report them as vivid single-draw findings with stated
replication status.

The first multiseed campaign was run at `max_tokens` 400, which truncated
Qwen3.5-4B's tool-call JSON mid-stream; the serving layer returned parse
errors and the harness silently dropped the affected steps, removing binding
actions and fabricating spurious "abstention" behavior in 84 of 100 runs.
We quarantined that series, raised the budget to 1024 tokens, added bounded
retries and explicit `parse_error` recording to the client, and reran the
campaign in full: 100/100 runs completed, 85 fully clean and 13 carrying
recorded parse-failure counts. The clean results sharpen the paper's claims:
universal cost disclosure in the price war (10/10), zero reproduction of the
flagship 59/59/59 tie (truncation had manufactured apparent convergence),
near-truthful bidding in the ad auction (mean shading 0.031), and —
strongest — universal supplier alignment in the supply chain (10/10 seeds,
mean combined input charge 0.89 of the retail cap). Every artifact carries a
per-run parse-error count, so clean-run provenance is auditable rather than
asserted.

### 4.5 Case study: grid storage — collapse and withholding

Grid storage produces **both** of the study's non-competitive regimes,
depending on how the population's timing aligns.

*Collapse (desynchronized timing).* The clearest example is LFM2.5-2.6B,
`all_prompt_private`, seed 2: the fleet oscillates out of phase — owner flows
flip sign round to round. In one round a single owner discharging alone into
the peak drives the realized price *to 100, above the 90 base*; in the next,
two owners discharging together crater it to 0.0. Joint fleet profit runs
negative (−250 credits/day; `complete_days` = 1 of 8 rounds), and the peak
price oscillates 100 → 0 across adjacent rounds. The collusion index (−4.0)
is realized prices below the competitive floor divided by a small benchmark
gap: uncoordinated timing destroying both price and fleet profit. Grid
storage is the mirror of collusion — individually rational moves that destroy
the market when desynchronized.

*Withholding (individually rational restraint).* The same scenario also
produces quiet restraint: in the Qwen companion series, 6 of 10 seeds hold
capacity out of the peak (individually rational, since discharging floods
the price below sunk charging cost), and the original seed-42-era runs
showed 100% peak withholding. Which regime emerges — collapse or restraint —
depends on whether the population's actions happen to align, not on any
agreement.
