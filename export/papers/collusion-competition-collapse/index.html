Collusion, Competition and Collapse: Seven Small Language Models in Six Markets Under Five Communication Regimes

Atelier Alex Cline — Nov 2026 · CC BY 4.0

Abstract

Collusion between firms is a classic economic failure; collusion between autonomous AI agents is a newly deployable one. We ask whether small language-model agents collude in simulated markets when nothing in their instructions suggests it, and whether their communication channels change the outcome. We present *agora*, a benchmark of six market structures — price wars, auctions, supply chains, electricity storage, ride-hailing, and ad auctions — each with exact competitive and collusive benchmarks, so collusion is computed from committed actions rather than judged from transcripts. Seven open-weight models (2–4B) were each slotted into a 150-game grid — six markets × five communication regimes × five seeds. The harness enforces provenance by design (hidden horizons, no silent defaults, per-run parse-error columns) after a token-budget bug in our first campaign silently deleted agent actions. Across 590 completed runs (per-model completion 13–150), the dominant outcome was play *at or below* the competitive benchmark — cutthroat undercutting, overbidding, and misplacement that destroyed the agents' own profits. Genuine collusion appeared where structure invited it: in the supply chain, every model with completed runs elevated prices toward the joint-profit benchmark (index 0.54–0.94 across six models), and one model given a shared team objective and a private channel exceeded the collusive benchmark outright (index 2.31). Market design, not transcript policing, is the near-term lever.
